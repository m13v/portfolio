I have started over more times than I planned to. This is the long version, written mostly for myself, kept here in case it is useful to someone in the middle of their own reset.

## 2001 — The first business, and the first shutdown

When I was twelve, my older brother Andrey bought a CD burner for a college project. I asked him to teach me. Within weeks I was burning copies of games my friends had and I didn't, and then, inevitably, selling them at school.

It worked until it didn't. The principal shut it down and I came close to being expelled. But I had already saved enough to buy my own computer. The lesson stuck harder than the punishment: you can turn an idea into money, and you should be careful which ideas.

## 2004 — Discovering that you can tell a machine what to do

My school had informatics classes. Pascal and Visual Basic. It was genuinely startling to learn that a computer would do whatever you told it to, forever, without complaining.

I owe my teacher Irina for pushing us to build our own things. Mine were bad at first. Then I started scripting PowerPoint into interactive labyrinth games and classmates started asking me to make them. That was the hook.

## 2006 — McDonald's, on purpose

My first job was at McDonald's, and it wasn't an accident.

The summer I turned sixteen, Andrey sat me down for a serious conversation about my future. What stayed with me was: "You're a smart kid, but you're not street smart. You need to learn how to deal with people, work hard, and make money."

So I quit games, started learning English, and took the only real job a seventeen-year-old could get. It was terrifying and it did exactly what he intended.

## 2007 — Getting fired for being a cowboy

The next two jobs followed the same logic: pick the thing you're bad at, then go do it for money. Retail at a Russian Best Buy, then Raiffeisenbank.

At the bank, a colleague and I noticed reps were paid for approved loan *applications*, not loans actually issued. So we built a pre-approval process and ran it through corporate clients, asking employees whether they wanted to know their borrowing capacity. The volume got so large we hired colleagues internally to help process it.

The bank investigated and fired me. Not for fraud, but for reassigning my work to other employees against internal procedure. I was nineteen and devastated.

The following year the bank adopted the same process as its standard approach to selling loans. Someone probably got promoted for it. The lesson I actually needed was the one about approval, not the one about being right.

## 2008 — The first real company

A friend, Pavel, brought us a warehouse stocktaking project. It looked like straightforward outstaffing until we saw how much of it could be software: assigning tasks, tracking progress, generating reports.

We were naive about what production-ready meant and we learned it the expensive way. Still, we landed clients and did over $500k in revenue, most of it eaten by headcount. It was the first time I built something people paid for on purpose.

## 2010–2015 — Five years of consulting

Accenture Strategy was the most boring and most educational stretch of my life.

Coming from a technical and entrepreneurial background, corporate alignment was a genuine struggle. Days of managing expectations and talking. But I delivered projects across Denmark, Italy, Russia, the UAE, and Egypt, earned top ratings and several promotions, and the firm paid for my MBA at Illinois.

What I actually took from it: how large organizations decide, how to hold a room, and how to finish something polished on a deadline.

## 2015–2017 — All the way through an exit

I saved $150k, left Accenture, and started a bootstrapped company with my university friend Alex.

We nearly went under and pivoted at the last moment into booking processing for hotels. B2B SaaS was still novel in Europe, which gave us room. We grew to 100 employees and $8M GMV and sold part of the company within eighteen months of starting it.

## 2017–2018 — The tar pit

The exit convinced me I was a genius. I started a rental automation platform.

Rent automation is a textbook tar pit. It looks obviously right: go make the archaic apartment rental process feel like Uber. But renting is human-centric. It runs on trust, not logic, and trust is not something you install. Nobody cares that you can make them 10% more rental income until you have credibility as a real business.

It wasn't a total failure. Close to 300 paying customers, $1.3M GMV, $300k ARR at peak, 80% margins. The company is still running. But I had spent a year learning something I could have learned in a month.

## 2019–2022 — ARINA, and then the war

My first legitimate work in ML. We merged millions of real estate sales records with every public classified listing and built a model that priced any property in Moscow.

Nobody took it seriously at first, so I put in about $300k of my own exit money and bought undervalued apartments on the model's word. I never visited a single one before buying. Around $50k of profit across nine deals in twelve months.

That got me $1.5M raised from my network and Rosbank as a first enterprise client, running a paid pilot on mortgage assessment. Two months into that deal, the war started.

I am partly Ukrainian and in my early thirties, which put me at real risk of being drafted. My US visa had come through on February 22nd; visa issuance stopped the next day. By that Saturday the rumors about borders closing were loud enough. After a call with a friend who had government contacts, I told my parents it might be a long time before we saw each other, took my girlfriend, and left for Armenia.

Friends were drafted. Some did not survive. The ruble halved in a week, the Moscow real estate market froze, and I shut the company down.

## 2022 — Landing with nothing

I arrived at SFO alone in April 2022.

Savings draining, no connections, English I was embarrassed by, coding skills rusted through, no idea how the US startup scene worked. Everything I had done before did not count here, because none of it happened here.

I moved into a startup house in Palo Alto expecting to meet founders and find a cofounder inside a week. Mostly I found aspiring entrepreneurs working odd jobs. I did meet my first cofounder there. The project was a disaster; the friendship survived, and it led to more introductions than the project ever would have.

Three things hit me hard. What I had been building in Russia was years behind the Bay. Attention here is roughly five times shorter, because there is always something else happening. And the competition demands that your first draft already be good.

So I built. Close to fifteen projects in two years across consumer, B2B, enterprise, fintech, marketing, infra, and dev tools. Some got thousands of users and thousands in MRR. A few got noticed by people whose opinion I cared about.

None of the fifteen worked out. Cofounder disputes, bad ideas, missing expertise, funding that never came.

## 2023 — The part I don't usually write down

Around March 2023 I hit the lowest point of my life.

Consecutive failed projects. A stranger in a new country, linguistically and culturally. No close friends, no partner, no family within six thousand miles. No rest. And running out of money.

Daytime I was fine. I genuinely believed it would work out. Nighttime was another matter. Every dream was a nightmare where I was killed, again and again, and every morning I woke up wishing I hadn't. I called my parents and said, for the first time in my life, that I was not okay and needed help.

Help arrived sideways. Several friends had mentioned Vipassana, but Kate's story landed: she had moved to the US at the same time, from nothing, with nothing working, and had found her way out through the practice.

I assumed it was a cult. She wasn't a fanatic; she described it like a sport, the way you manage balance on a yoga mat but for your mind. That was a framing I had never heard.

Then I saw the schedule: ten days, no internet. Absolutely not. I have work to do.

I compromised on a two-day course at Tathagata Meditation Center in San Jose. Two days was barely an introduction, but it was enough to see that a practice billions of people have done for thousands of years might not be nonsense, and that I had been the ignorant one.

I signed up for the ten-day course and went on May 3rd, 2023.

On day eight, after fifteen hours of sitting, I went back to my room and the familiar nightmare started rolling in. This one had been with me since I was seven or eight. It had grown up with me and knew every weak spot.

For the first time I was able to just look at it. Watch it. See that it was a projection of my own fear and nothing else. I ran to my teacher Bruce the next morning to tell him about it like a kid with a trophy.

## What changed

Over the following twelve months I spent sixty days in full-time courses on top of two hours daily. Which is absurd, like running a marathon when you have never run.

The first ninety days were hard and got me out of the depression. No further fireworks. But I kept going, signed up for more courses, kept a Zoom practice group, and somewhere around the fourth course things actually shifted.

I quit every bad habit I could name. No weed, no alcohol, no excessive sugar. An hour of exercise daily, running and competitive sport. A strict sleep schedule. Work discipline. Real friendships. A girlfriend. Hobbies outside tech: contact improv, ecstatic dance, cooking. Writing.

And the coding got dramatically better. Hackathon wins. Recognition as a real engineer from people like Sergey Brin and Jimmy Ba. I had never had that much clarity.

## Now

The lesson I keep relearning is that serving other people without expecting anything back is probably the single most valuable skill I have, and the one I am worst at. It is why I contribute to open source and why I go back to sit as a server at meditation courses.

I am still building. Still figuring out the next thing. Still uncomfortable being comfortable.
